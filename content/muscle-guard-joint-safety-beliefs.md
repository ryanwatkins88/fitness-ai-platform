## Internal beliefs (what they believe about their own body)

### 1. "I can't lift weights because of my bad shoulder/knee/back."
**Hook:** "'I can't lift because of my shoulder' is the belief keeping you from the exact thing that would help it."
**Caption:** A bad shoulder doesn't rule out strength training — it rules out the wrong exercises, done the wrong way. This is exactly what I work with day to day: I train retired and 50+ clients through shoulder impingement and rotator cuff issues regularly. There's almost always a way in, just not the generic one.

### 2. "Lifting will make my arthritis worse."
**Hook:** "You think lifting will make your arthritis worse. Done right, it's one of the best things for it."
**Caption:** Joint-friendly resistance training is one of the most researched tools for managing osteoarthritis pain and function — the key word is "joint-friendly," which most generic programs don't know how to build. That's exactly the population I train regularly: the right moves, in the right order, so training helps the joint instead of aggravating it.

### 3. "My bones are too thin (osteoporosis) — too risky to lift."
**Hook:** "If you've been told your bones are too thin to lift, that's backwards — thinning bones are one of the strongest reasons to lift, done correctly."
**Caption:** Resistance training is one of the few things that actually helps maintain bone density — avoiding it altogether often lets osteoporosis progress faster, not slower. The real requirement isn't "don't lift," it's "lift with the right loading and form for that condition," which is the exact kind of programming I build for clients regularly.

### 4. "I was told to avoid weights after my injury (rotator cuff / meniscus / sciatica)."
**Hook:** "'Avoid weights' after an injury is often temporary advice that outlives its shelf life."
**Caption:** Early after an injury, avoiding load makes sense. Years later, that same advice can turn into unnecessary avoidance of strength training your body could actually handle again. I train people back into strength work after rotator cuff repairs, meniscus repairs, and sciatica regularly — the goal isn't ignoring the injury, it's building around it correctly.

### 5. "I had a stroke / have fibromyalgia, so normal workouts aren't safe for me."
**Hook:** "A 'normal' workout might not be safe for you. That doesn't mean no workout is."
**Caption:** Conditions like fibromyalgia, Parkinson's, or post-stroke recovery need real modification, not a blanket "don't exercise" rule — appropriately scaled resistance and balance training is something I coach regularly. The plan has to change. The idea that training isn't possible at all usually isn't true.

### 6. "I'll just make things worse trying to exercise on my own."
**Hook:** "You're probably right that winging it could make things worse. That's different from training being off the table."
**Caption:** That instinct is smart, not fearful — generic programs really can aggravate a real injury or condition. The fix isn't avoiding training, it's not doing it alone, and having someone who works with these conditions daily build the plan with you.

### 7. "I need a physical therapist, not a personal trainer — this isn't for me."
**Hook:** "You think you need a PT, not a trainer. What if you needed someone who lives in that exact handoff?"
**Caption:** A lot of people stall out in the gap between "finished physical therapy" and "safe to train normally again" — that gap is literally where I work: helping people transition out of PT into real, ongoing strength training, not just once, but as a regular part of what I do. You don't have to choose between rehab-level caution and real strength training.

## Internal beliefs (what they believe about their own body)

### 1. "I can't lift weights because of my bad shoulder/knee/back."
**Hook:** "'I can't lift because of my shoulder' is the belief keeping you from the exact thing that would help it."
**Caption:** A bad shoulder doesn't rule out strength training — it rules out the wrong exercises, done the wrong way. As a Physical Therapist Assistant, this is the part of my background that isn't just theory; I've coached people through actual rotator cuff tears back into training safely. There's almost always a way in, just not the generic one.

### 2. "Lifting will make my arthritis worse."
**Hook:** "You think lifting will make your arthritis worse. Done right, it's one of the best things for it."
**Caption:** Joint-friendly resistance training is one of the most researched tools for managing osteoarthritis pain and function — the key word is "joint-friendly," which most generic programs don't know how to build. That's the exact gap my background fills: the right moves, in the right order, so training helps the joint instead of aggravating it.

### 3. "My bones are too thin (osteoporosis) — too risky to lift."
**Hook:** "If you've been told your bones are too thin to lift, that's backwards — thinning bones are one of the strongest reasons to lift, done correctly."
**Caption:** Resistance training is one of the few things that actually helps maintain bone density — avoiding it altogether often lets osteoporosis progress faster, not slower. The real requirement isn't "don't lift," it's "lift with the right loading and form for that condition," which is exactly what a Physical Therapist Assistant background is built for.

### 4. "I was told to avoid weights after my injury (rotator cuff / meniscus / sciatica)."
**Hook:** "'Avoid weights' after an injury is often temporary advice that outlives its shelf life."
**Caption:** Early after an injury, avoiding load makes sense. Years later, that same advice can turn into unnecessary avoidance of strength training your body could actually handle again. I've coached people back into training after rotator cuff tears, meniscus tears, and sciatica — the goal isn't ignoring the injury, it's building around it correctly.

### 5. "I had a stroke / have fibromyalgia, so normal workouts aren't safe for me."
**Hook:** "A 'normal' workout might not be safe for you. That doesn't mean no workout is."
**Caption:** Conditions like fibromyalgia or post-stroke recovery need real modification, not a blanket "don't exercise" rule — appropriately scaled resistance training is something I've coached clients through directly. The plan has to change. The idea that training isn't possible at all usually isn't true.

### 6. "I'll just make things worse trying to exercise on my own."
**Hook:** "You're probably right that winging it could make things worse. That's different from training being off the table."
**Caption:** That instinct is smart, not fearful — generic programs really can aggravate a real injury or condition. The fix isn't avoiding training, it's not doing it alone. That's the entire reason to have someone with a clinical background in the room, even virtually.

### 7. "I need a physical therapist, not a personal trainer — this isn't for me."
**Hook:** "You think you need a PT, not a trainer. What if you could get both, in one person?"
**Caption:** A lot of people stall out in the gap between "finished physical therapy" and "safe to train normally again" — that gap is exactly where a trainer with a Physical Therapist Assistant background is built to work. You don't have to choose between rehab-level care and real strength training.

## External beliefs (what they believe about the fitness/medical system)

### 8. "Generic programs don't account for real injuries or conditions."
**Hook:** "You're right that most programs weren't built for your joints. This one was."
**Caption:** Most fitness content is written for people without a real injury history — that's a fair thing to be skeptical of. Coaching people through torn rotator cuffs, sciatica, osteoarthritis, meniscus tears, osteoporosis, fibromyalgia, and post-stroke recovery isn't a marketing line here, it's actual coaching history.

### 9. "Trainers aren't qualified to work with medical conditions — only PTs are."
**Hook:** "You've been told trainers and medical conditions don't mix. That line gets blurry with the right background."
**Caption:** A general trainer without clinical training probably shouldn't be programming around a torn meniscus or osteoporosis — that skepticism is fair. A Physical Therapist Assistant background is exactly what closes that gap: real experience with real conditions, not just certifications on paper.

### 10. "If I had a real medical condition, a fitness influencer couldn't help me."
**Hook:** "You assume 'social media coach' means 'not qualified for real conditions.' Worth checking who's actually behind the account first."
**Caption:** Totally fair assumption most of the time — a lot of fitness content online is exactly that. This one happens to come from someone who's also a certified personal trainer through NASM and a Physical Therapist Assistant, with real coaching history in rotator cuff tears, sciatica, osteoarthritis, meniscus tears, osteoporosis, fibromyalgia, and post-stroke recovery. Worth a closer look before ruling it out.

### 11. "You need to be fully healed before you can strength train again."
**Hook:** "'Wait until you're fully healed' sometimes means waiting years longer than necessary."
**Caption:** For a lot of joint and injury cases, appropriately scaled strength training is part of getting fully healed, not something to wait for after. The right sequencing matters more than waiting indefinitely — that's a coaching judgment call, not a generic rule.

### 12. "There's no safe way to lift with osteoporosis/arthritis — it's just risky."
**Hook:** "'No safe way to lift with that condition' usually means 'no one showed you the safe way yet.'"
**Caption:** Osteoporosis and arthritis change what safe lifting looks like — they don't eliminate it. The research supports resistance training for both conditions when it's programmed correctly, which is the actual skill being offered here, not a blanket "just be careful."
